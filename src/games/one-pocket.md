    definition: The one corner pocket at the foot of the table assigned to you. Only balls dropped there score for you.
  - term: Neutral pocket
    definition: The four pockets belonging to neither player. Balls pocketed there are spotted, not scored.
  - term: Owed ball
    definition: A foul penalty a player cannot yet pay because they have no scored balls to give back.
  - term: Behind the line
    definition: Behind the head string. All ball in hand in one-pocket is restricted to this area.

    definition: A shot played off one or more cushions, the staple of the game since your pocket is rarely in line.

## Equipment and the rack

- **Table.** Six pockets. The game is usually played on a 9 ft table.
- **Balls.** All fifteen object balls plus a cue ball.
- **The rack.** All fifteen racked tightly in **no particular order**, apex ball as near as possible to the foot spot.
- **The pockets.** The two corner pockets at the **foot** of the table are the scoring pockets, one per player. The other four — both side pockets and both head corners — are **neutral**.

## Object of the game

Legally pocket **eight balls into your own pocket**. The first player to do so wins.

There are fifteen balls and sixteen points of scoring available between two players who need eight each — so one ball, somewhere in the game, is usually the whole match.

## Choosing pockets and the break

- **Before breaking, the breaker chooses** one of the two foot corner pockets. The opponent is assigned the other by default.
- The break is played with **cue ball in hand behind the head string**.
- The cue ball may reach the rack directly or off one or more cushions.
- After contacting the rack, either a ball must be pocketed, or **the cue ball or at least one object ball must reach a rail**. Otherwise it is a foul, carrying the usual one-ball penalty.

A good break in one-pocket does not try to score. It nudges a ball or two toward your own pocket and leaves the cue ball safe at the far end.

## Scoring

**One-pocket is not a call-shot game.** Nothing is nominated, and how a ball arrives makes no difference — banks, kisses, combinations and outright flukes all count the same.

| Where the ball drops | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Your own pocket | Scores one for you |
| Your opponent's pocket | **Scores one for them**, even though you shot it |
| Any of the four neutral pockets | Spotted, scores nothing — see rule 7 |
| Off the table | Foul, and the ball is spotted |

> This is the rule that makes one-pocket what it is. You can win a game on a ball your opponent shot, and you can hand them the match with a stray bank. No other pool game lets your opponent score for you.

A player who legally pockets a ball in their own pocket continues at the table.

If the cue ball scratches or jumps the table on a stroke that also pockets a ball in the opponent's pocket, that ball is **spotted instead of scored**.

## Fouls and the one-ball penalty

Every foul ends the inning and costs **one ball**: one of the offender's already-scored balls is taken off their tally and spotted on the foot spot, as described in rule 7.

- Failing to meet the break requirements above.
- Failing to drive any ball to a rail after contact, when nothing is pocketed.
- Scratching, or driving the cue ball off the table.
- Driving any ball off the table.
- Touching a ball with cue, hand or clothing; a double hit or a push shot.
- Shooting before all balls have come to rest, or out of turn.
- Having both feet off the floor at the moment of striking.

**Where the incoming player shoots from is unusual, and worth learning:**

- After a **scratch, or the cue ball leaving the table**, they take **ball in hand — but only behind the head string**. Never anywhere on the table, as in eight- and nine-ball.
- After **any other foul**, the cue ball is played **exactly where it lies**.

That second case is why one-pocket safety play is so punishing: most fouls leave your opponent looking at the same horrible cue ball you were.

## Owed balls

A player who fouls with **no scored balls to give back** cannot pay the penalty. They **owe** a ball instead, and a marker is set aside to record it.

The debt is repaid out of the next balls they score, at the end of the first inning in which they score, before those balls count towards their eight.

## Spotting

Everything that is spotted goes to the **foot spot** — the spot the apex ball was racked on, not the centre of the table.

- If the foot spot is taken, the ball goes to the nearest free space **on the long string directly below it**, towards the foot rail.
- If that line fills all the way to the rail, spotting continues **above** the foot spot along the same line.
- A spotted ball is placed **frozen tight** against any object ball already on that line, without disturbing it. If the **cue ball** is what blocks the line, the spotted ball goes close to it but **not** frozen.

Spotting is done at the **end of the offending player's inning**, not the instant a ball is owed — so several balls may go back up at once.

> If the table is cleared and nobody has reached eight, every pocketed ball is spotted and play simply continues. The game cannot end in an empty table.

## Three consecutive fouls

**Three fouls in a row loses the game.** The opponent or the referee must warn the player that they are **on two fouls** before the third shot is played.

## Winning

The first player to have **eight balls legally scored in their own pocket** wins — whether they pocketed the last one themselves or their opponent did it for them.
